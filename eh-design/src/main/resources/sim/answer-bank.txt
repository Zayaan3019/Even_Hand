# Written-answer bank for the course simulator.

# Each question has rubric points (+), each written three ways, as different students would put
# the same idea; misconceptions (-), which cost half a point each; and neutral filler (~). A
# simulated student makes each point with a chance that rises with their ability, may state a

# answer deserves is computed from the points it actually makes, so the text and the mark agree
# the way they do on a real script. Variants are separated by " | ".

## Q beam-moment | Where is the bending moment maximum in a simply supported beam, and how do you find it?
+ The bending moment is maximum where the shear force is zero or changes sign. | Maximum BM occurs at the section where the shear force passes through zero. | Wherever the SF diagram crosses the axis, the moment reaches its peak.
+ For a central point load P on span L the maximum moment is PL/4 at midspan. | Under a central load the peak moment equals P times L over 4, at the centre. | With P at the middle, M max = PL/4 at L/2.
+ For a uniformly distributed load w the maximum moment is wL^2/8 at midspan. | A UDL of intensity w gives M max = wL²/8 at the centre of the span. | Under UDL the moment is largest at mid span and equals w L squared by 8.
+ The reactions are found first from the equilibrium equations, sum of forces and moments equal to zero. | First find the support reactions using ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0. | Start by taking moments about one support to get the reactions.
+ The moment at a section is the sum of moments of all forces on one side of it. | M at any section is obtained by summing the moments of the forces to its left (or right). | Cut the beam at the section and take moments of everything on one side.
- The bending moment is maximum where the shear force is maximum. | Peak moment is at the point of maximum shear.
- The maximum moment always occurs at the supports. | For a simply supported beam the moment is largest at the supports.
~ This is an important concept in structural analysis. | Hence the answer. | The SFD and BMD can be drawn to show this.

## Q truss-member | Find the force in member BC of the truss and state whether it is in tension or compression.
+ Use the method of sections, cutting through BC and two other members. | Pass a section through members BC, CF and EF and consider one part. | The method of sections is quickest: cut the truss through BC.
+ Take moments about the joint where the other two cut members meet, so BC is the only unknown. | Moments about F eliminate the other two unknown member forces. | Choosing the moment centre at the intersection of the other members isolates F_BC.
+ The force in BC works out to 25 kN. | F_BC = 25 kN. | Solving gives a member force of 25 kN in BC.
+ Member BC is in tension, since the force pulls away from the joints. | BC is a tension member. | The positive sign means BC is in tension.
+ Check with the method of joints at joint B. | The result can be verified by equilibrium of joint B. | A joint equilibrium check at B confirms the value.
- Member BC is in compression. | The force in BC is compressive.
- The force in BC is 50 kN. | F_BC comes out as 50 kN.
~ Trusses are pin jointed and members carry only axial force. | Assuming all joints are pins. | Neglecting self weight of members.

## Q effective-stress | State the principle of effective stress and explain its significance for soils.
+ Effective stress is total stress minus pore water pressure, σ' = σ − u. | σ' equals the total stress minus the pore pressure. | The effective stress is the total stress less the water pressure in the pores.
+ The strength and deformation of soil are controlled by effective stress, not total stress. | Shear strength and compressibility depend on σ'. | Soil behaviour, both strength and volume change, is governed by effective stress.
+ Effective stress is carried by the soil skeleton through grain to grain contacts. | It represents the stress transmitted through the solid particles. | σ' is the intergranular stress borne by the soil grains.
+ A rise in pore pressure reduces effective stress and can cause failure, as in liquefaction. | Increasing u lowers σ', which may lead to slope failure or liquefaction. | When pore pressure builds up the soil loses strength.
+ The principle was proposed by Terzaghi. | This is Terzaghi's principle. | Karl Terzaghi stated it in 1923.
- Effective stress is total stress plus pore water pressure. | σ' = σ + u.
- Pore water pressure increases the strength of soil. | Higher pore pressure makes the soil stronger.
~ Soil is a three phase material. | This is the most important principle in soil mechanics. | It applies to saturated soils.

## Q reynolds | What is the Reynolds number, and how does it decide whether pipe flow is laminar or turbulent?
+ The Reynolds number is the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces. | Re compares inertia forces with viscous forces. | It is a dimensionless ratio of inertial to viscous effects.
+ Re = ρVD/μ, where V is the mean velocity and D the pipe diameter. | It is defined as density times velocity times diameter divided by dynamic viscosity. | Re = VD/ν using the kinematic viscosity.
+ In a pipe the flow is laminar when Re is below about 2000. | For Re < 2000 the flow is laminar. | Low Reynolds numbers, under roughly 2300, give laminar flow.
+ Flow is turbulent above about 4000, with a transition region in between. | Re > 4000 means turbulent flow; between 2000 and 4000 it is transitional. | Beyond about 4000 the flow becomes turbulent.
+ In laminar flow the friction factor is 64/Re. | For laminar flow f = 64/Re. | The Darcy friction factor in laminar flow equals 64 divided by Re.
- Flow is turbulent when the Reynolds number is low. | Low Re gives turbulent flow.
- The Reynolds number is the ratio of viscous forces to gravity forces. | Re compares viscosity with gravity.
~ It was introduced by Osborne Reynolds. | It is used in fluid mechanics. | This is a dimensionless number.

## Q first-law | State the first law of thermodynamics for a closed system and apply it to a cycle.
+ For a closed system, the heat added equals the change in internal energy plus the work done, Q = ΔU + W. | Q − W = ΔU for a closed system. | Energy supplied as heat goes into raising internal energy and doing work.
+ Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it only changes form. | The first law is the law of conservation of energy. | It states that energy is conserved.
+ For a cycle the change in internal energy is zero, so net heat equals net work. | Over a complete cycle ΔU = 0, so ∮δQ = ∮δW. | In a cyclic process the net heat transfer equals the net work.
+ Internal energy is a property and a point function, while heat and work are path functions. | U depends only on the state; Q and W depend on the path. | Heat and work are not properties, but internal energy is.
+ A perpetual motion machine of the first kind is impossible. | No machine can produce work without an energy input (PMM1). | This rules out a perpetual motion machine of the first kind.
- Heat and work are properties of the system. | Work is a point function.
- In a cycle the net work is always zero. | The net work done in a cycle is zero.
~ Thermodynamics deals with energy interactions. | Sign convention: heat added is positive. | This is very important for engines.

## Q traverse | How are closing errors in a closed traverse adjusted?

+ The Bowditch rule distributes the error in proportion to the length of each line. | Bowditch's method corrects each line in proportion to its length. | By the compass rule, corrections are proportional to line lengths.
+ The transit rule distributes it in proportion to the latitudes and departures. | Transit rule corrections are proportional to the latitude and departure of each line. | The transit method uses the magnitude of latitudes and departures.
+ Bowditch is used when angles and distances are measured with equal precision. | The compass rule suits traverses where linear and angular measurements are equally accurate. | Use Bowditch when angles and lengths have similar precision.
+ The relative precision is the closing error divided by the perimeter. | Accuracy is expressed as closing error over total length of the traverse. | The ratio of error to perimeter gives the precision, such as 1 in 5000.
- The error is added to the last line only. | All the error is put on the final leg.

## Q unit-hydrograph | What is a unit hydrograph and what assumptions does it rest on?
+ A unit hydrograph is the direct runoff hydrograph from one unit of effective rainfall over the catchment. | It is the hydrograph of direct runoff from 1 cm of excess rainfall. | The UH is the runoff response to a unit depth of effective rainfall.
+ It is for rainfall of a specified duration occurring uniformly over the catchment. | The rainfall is of a given duration and uniform in space and time. | It is defined for a D-hour storm spread evenly over the basin.
+ It assumes linearity: ordinates are proportional to the effective rainfall. | Runoff ordinates scale in proportion to the excess rainfall (linearity). | Doubling the excess rain doubles every ordinate.
+ It assumes time invariance: the same storm always gives the same hydrograph. | The response does not change with time (time invariance). | The catchment's behaviour is assumed constant over time.
+ Hydrographs of longer storms are built by superposition, or with the S-curve method. | Superposition of lagged unit hydrographs gives the response to complex storms. | The S-curve converts a UH from one duration to another.
- A unit hydrograph includes the base flow. | Base flow is part of the unit hydrograph.
- The unit hydrograph is valid for any catchment size. | It applies equally to very large basins.
~ Hydrology is important for design of dams. | Rainfall data is collected from rain gauges. | The peak is important.

## Q sight-distance | What is stopping sight distance and what does it depend on?
+ Stopping sight distance is the distance needed for a driver to stop safely after seeing an obstruction. | SSD is the minimum distance required to bring a vehicle to rest before an obstacle. | It is the distance a driver needs to perceive a hazard and stop.
+ It is the sum of the lag distance during perception-reaction time and the braking distance. | SSD = lag distance + braking distance. | It has two parts: distance covered while reacting, and distance while braking.
+ The lag distance is v times t, with a reaction time of about 2.5 seconds. | Lag distance = vt, taking t as 2.5 s per IRC. | During the reaction time of 2.5 s the vehicle travels v t.
+ The braking distance is v^2/(2gf), where f is the coefficient of friction. | Braking distance equals v²/2gf. | Braking takes v squared over 2 g f.
+ A steeper downgrade increases the stopping distance. | On a descending gradient the braking distance is longer. | Gradient matters: downhill increases SSD.
- Stopping sight distance does not depend on speed. | SSD is the same at all speeds.
- Braking distance is v/(2gf). | The braking distance is proportional to speed.
~ IRC gives standards for highways. | Safety is very important on roads. | Visibility matters.

## Q damping | What is damping in a vibrating structure, and what is critical damping?
+ Damping is the dissipation of energy that makes vibrations die out over time. | It is the mechanism by which vibrating energy is lost, so the amplitude decays. | Damping removes energy and reduces the amplitude of vibration.
+ Critical damping is the smallest damping for which the system returns to rest without oscillating. | At critical damping the system comes back to equilibrium fastest without oscillation. | Critically damped systems do not oscillate and settle quickly.
+ The critical damping coefficient is c_c = 2√(km) = 2mω_n. | c critical equals 2 m ω_n. | It is given by 2 times the square root of k m.
+ The damping ratio is ζ = c/c_c; buildings typically have about 5% damping. | ζ is the ratio of actual to critical damping, around 0.05 for structures. | Most structures are underdamped with ζ near 5 percent.
+ An underdamped system oscillates with a decaying amplitude at the damped frequency ω_d = ω_n√(1 − ζ²). | For ζ < 1 the motion oscillates and decays. | Underdamped vibration has ω_d = ω_n sqrt(1 − ζ²).
- Critical damping makes the system oscillate forever. | A critically damped system vibrates with constant amplitude.
- Damping increases the natural frequency. | With more damping the system vibrates faster.
~ Vibrations are important in earthquake engineering. | A spring mass system is the simplest model. | Damping is present in all real structures.

## Q eigen | What are eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix, and how are they found?

+ The sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace and their product equals the determinant. | Trace = sum of λ, determinant = product of λ. | Σλ = tr A and Πλ = det A.

- An eigenvector can be the zero vector. | The zero vector is an eigenvector.

## Q recursion | Explain recursion with the example of computing a factorial, and its cost.

~ Python and C both support recursion. | Recursion is elegant. | Iteration can also be used.
